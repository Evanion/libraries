# One policy behind a screen

Every control on a screen is one `can`, and the policy is the only place the
rule behind it is written. This page works one shop screen end to end: a game
listing, three people who might be looking at it, and an action bar whose
buttons are decisions rather than markup.

The three setup tiers built policies and read decisions out of them. What
changes here is only where the answers go: into an interface, one control at a
time, with nothing between the policy and the pixel.

## What the bar draws for each shopper

The action bar renders one button per action and asks `access.can` for each. A
`Decision` is what decides whether the button is drawn, and `reason` is what
decides what it says when it is drawn and disabled.

For the bookseller above, `review` and `edit` come back `allowed`, `publish`
comes back `no-rule-matched`. Three answers, three controls, and the component
holds no condition of its own — it holds a list of actions.

That is the property worth taking away: adding a fourth control to the bar is a
statement in the policy and a row in the list. It is never a branch in the
component, because a branch in the component is a rule written somewhere the
policy cannot see.

## A status change moves the bar without touching the policy

Press Publish and `object.status` becomes `published`. The deny in the shop's
listing policy now matches, `edit` comes back `denied`, and the Edit button
leaves the bar for every role — the bookseller's and the owner's alike, because
a deny outranks a matching allow.

Nothing was recomputed by hand and no second rule was written for the published
case. One row changed and four statements produced a different screen.

## The browser's copy of the shop's policy decides nothing

The decisions this screen renders are convenience. The same `can` runs again in
the shop's server runtime, over a subject that runtime resolved itself, before
any listing is edited or published. A screen that hid the button does not excuse
the handler the button posts to. [The security contract](/acl/security) is the
long form, and [Platforms](/acl/platforms) is where the second call sits on each
stack.

## Where a refused control gets its wording

`Decision` carries `reason`, `blockedBy`, `cause` and `missing` beside
`allowed`, and a bar that greys a control out rather than removing it reads
them. What each key means, and which of them are repairable, is [Why was this
refused?](/acl/refusals); the shape itself is in the [API
reference](/acl/api#decisions).
